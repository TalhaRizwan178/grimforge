# Venom in My Veins

**Genre:** Forbidden Romance / Dark Thriller
**Setting:** Ashveil — a city fracturing under the silent war of two criminal dynasties

---

## Plot

Ashveil does not have peace. It has a ceasefire — a thin, brittle thing held together by fear and the memory of the last war's body count. On one side of that line stands the Wolf Clan, sovereign of the northern districts, built on dominance and unbroken loyalty. On the other, the Serpent Syndicate, coiled through the southern quarters, trading in secrets, poison, and patience. They do not share streets. They do not share air. They do not share anything — until they do.
Dragneel is not yet the Wolf. He is the Wolf's son — the heir being sharpened like a blade, trained since boyhood to one day lead a dynasty soaked in blood and purpose. He carries the weight of that inheritance in his jaw, in the way he enters a room, in the silence that follows his name. He has been told since childhood that the Serpents are the enemy. He has seen what that enmity costs. He believes it completely. He has never had reason not to.
Liliana is being made into something colder than a weapon. She is the Syndicate's future — groomed by a father who never once confused affection with strategy. She has learned to read threats before they speak and neutralize them before they move. She does not fear the Wolf Clan; she catalogs them. Dragneel appears in her assessments as a variable: dangerous, ascending, worth monitoring. Nothing more.
They meet at the boundary — a negotiation neither of them chose, brokered by their fathers as a show of controlled tension. Liliana sits across the table and sees an heir, a liability, a future adversary. She takes her notes. She leaves.
Dragneel sees something else entirely.
He does not understand it at first. It arrives without warning — not as attraction so much as collision, a force that does not ask permission. He finds himself tracking details he has no strategic reason to track: the way she speaks without softening her words, the stillness she holds in a room full of people performing composure, the fact that she looked at him and saw through him without blinking. He tells himself it is tactical awareness. He is lying to himself.
She does not look back. That is the beginning of his ruin.
The city conspires to put them in proximity — shared territory disputes, forced parley, moments where the ceasefire demands their presence in the same room. Each time, Liliana is exactly what she always was: precise, unreadable, unbothered. Each time, Dragneel walks away knowing a little more about how it feels to want something that does not want him back. He is the future leader of the Wolf Clan. He has never wanted for anything. He has never wanted anything like this.
His father begins to notice the distraction. His lieutenants whisper. The Clan does not tolerate softness, least of all toward a Serpent. Dragneel knows this. He tells himself it does not matter — that what he feels is manageable, temporary, something that can be controlled like everything else he has ever been trained to control. The Wolf is supposed to be the one who hunts.
He is beginning to understand what it means to be prey to himself.
Liliana remains entirely unaware of the weight she carries in his mind. She catalogs Dragneel as a threat that is growing more capable with each passing month — nothing personal, simply an accurate reading of power. She has other concerns. She always has other concerns. The Syndicate is her inheritance and her cage, and she tends to it with the same disciplined hands she uses for everything: precise, purposeful, and without a single wasted feeling.
Between them lies a city that will not survive another war, two dynasties that have never once chosen peace, and a love that exists on one side only — vast, irrational, and destined to cost Dragneel everything the Wolf Clan ever built.
The feeling is his alone. The danger belongs to both of them. And the ending was written long before either of them drew their first breath — by people who were enemies before their children even had names.
